Write-Up

Motivation and Dataset

Climate change can feel distant when it is shown only as a global average, so we wanted to make the projections easier to connect to places people recognize in California. Our question is how much warming four major California cities may experience under different emissions futures. We used a subset of the CMIP6 climate model data, focused on annual near surface air temperature from the CESM2 model. The data includes Los Angeles, Sacramento, San Diego, and San Francisco, with yearly temperatures and temperature change compared with a 1995 to 2014 baseline. We also used GeoJSON boundary files for California and the four city polygons so the climate values could be shown directly on a map.

Transformations

We started with annual temperature records for each city, year, and climate scenario. To keep the visualization focused, we filtered the data to three SSP futures: SSP1 2.6, SSP2 4.5, and SSP5 8.5. Instead of mapping raw temperature alone, we used the temperature anomaly column, which shows how many degrees Celsius each city is above or below its 1995 to 2014 baseline. This makes the cities easier to compare because the color represents warming relative to each city's own recent climate. In the map data, we filtered the city boundary GeoJSON to only Los Angeles, Sacramento, San Diego, and San Francisco, then joined each city polygon to the matching climate record for the selected year and scenario.

Design Rationale

We chose a map because the main question is about how warming differs across places in California. The city boundary polygons make the visualization feel more specific than using only points, while still keeping the view simple enough to compare the four cities. Color is used to show warming severity, with darker and warmer colors representing larger temperature increases. The three buttons for low, middle, and high emissions let viewers switch between possible climate futures. The year slider lets viewers choose a year from 2015 to 2100, which makes it easier to see how warming builds over time. We also added tooltips so viewers can get the exact warming value, temperature, baseline, year, and scenario without crowding the map with too much text.

Development Process

We started by narrowing the project to one main question about projected warming in California cities. After choosing the CMIP6 temperature data, we cleaned the dataset into a smaller CSV with the cities, years, scenarios, and temperature anomalies needed for the visualization. The next step was connecting the climate data to the city boundary GeoJSON files. We used D3 to draw the map, set up the geographic projection, load the CSV and GeoJSON files, and update the colors when the viewer changes the year or emissions future. Most of the development time went into getting the map projection, color scale, slider, buttons, and tooltips to work together. We also spent time revising the design so the interaction stayed focused and the visualization did not try to show too many things at once. Work was divided between data preparation, D3 map implementation, interaction design, and writeup editing. In total, we spent about 5 people hours, with the most time going toward cleaning the data, joining it to the GeoJSON boundaries, and making the slider, buttons, and tooltips update smoothly.

Data sources: CMIP6 climate model temperature projections and GeoJSON boundary files for California and selected California cities.
