Write-Up

Motivation and Dataset

Climate change can feel distant when it is shown only as a global average, so we wanted to make the projections easier to connect to places people recognize across the United States. Our question is how severe projected warming becomes for major U.S. cities under different emissions futures. We used a subset of the CMIP6 climate model data, focused on annual near-surface air temperature from the CESM2 model. The data includes 50 major U.S. cities, with yearly temperatures and temperature change compared with a 1995 to 2014 baseline. We also used a U.S. states GeoJSON file as the map reference layer so the city-level climate values could be shown in their geographic context.

Transformations

We started with annual temperature records for each city, year, and climate scenario. To keep the visualization focused, we filtered the data to three SSP futures: SSP1 2.6, SSP2 4.5, and SSP5 8.5. Instead of mapping raw temperature alone, we used the temperature anomaly column, which shows how many degrees Celsius each city is above or below its 1995 to 2014 baseline. This makes the cities easier to compare because the color represents warming relative to each city's own recent climate. The final CSV has one row per city, year, and scenario, giving 12,900 city-pathway-year records. In D3, each selected year and scenario is joined to the city's latitude and longitude, projected onto the U.S. map, and drawn as a bubble whose color and radius both come from the same anomaly value.

Design Rationale

We chose a map because the main question is about how warming differs across places. A bubble map lets us show many more cities than a boundary map while keeping the page focused on one visual question. Color is used to show warming severity, with darker and warmer colors representing larger temperature increases. Bubble size encodes the same severity value, so the most extreme city projections become easier to notice at a glance. The three buttons for low, middle, and high emissions let viewers switch between possible climate futures. The year slider lets viewers choose a year from 2015 to 2100, which makes it easier to see how warming builds over time. We labeled only the hottest cities for the selected view and used tooltips for the exact city, warming, baseline, year, and scenario values.

Development Process

We started by narrowing the project to one main question about projected warming in recognizable cities. After choosing the CMIP6 temperature data, we cleaned the dataset into a smaller CSV with the cities, years, scenarios, and temperature anomalies needed for the visualization. We first prototyped a smaller California version, then expanded the design to 50 major U.S. cities after deciding that transparent circles would scale better than city polygons. We used D3 to draw the U.S. map, set up the geographic projection, load the CSV and GeoJSON files, and update the circles when the viewer changes the year or emissions future. Most of the development time went into preparing the climate subset, choosing an encoding that stayed readable with many cities, and tuning the color scale, size scale, slider, buttons, labels, and tooltips so the visualization stayed focused.

Data sources: CMIP6 CESM2 near-surface air temperature projections and a U.S. states GeoJSON reference layer.
